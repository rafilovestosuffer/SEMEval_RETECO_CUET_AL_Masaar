# Fusion is published, feasibility is the real constraint

**Your planned contribution H2 — decompose, retrieve per step, fuse the ranked lists — is already published twice over, and the honest novelty now lives in the setting rather than the technique.** MMLF (Findings of NAACL 2025) fuses sub-query retrievals with RRF and reports nDCG@10 gains on BEIR ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.367/)), and ReDI ran precisely the sum-vs-max-vs-RRF ablation you planned, finding **sum > max > RRF > concatenation** on BRIGHT nDCG@10 ([arXiv 2509.06544](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06544v1)). A third paper argues decomposition at first-stage retrieval actively *harms* ranking through semantic dilution ([arXiv 2606.08577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08577)). Meanwhile two of the five RETECO organizers published a CIKM '26 method that fuses reasoning *views* on TEMPO itself, and its learned difficulty gate buys only about **+0.005 nDCG@10 over equal-weight fusion at matched K** ([arXiv 2608.08940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08940)) — a strong hint that fusion weighting is a nearly exhausted seam. Against that, the operational picture is worse than the research picture: the recommended 4B retriever implies roughly **115–150 T4-hours to embed 1.65M documents** against a ~30 GPU-hour weekly quota, plus an **8.5 GB** embedding cache, so the first-stage model question is really a throughput question. And one thing must be resolved before any of this matters: **the notes contain a direct, unreconciled conflict about whether the official nDCG@10 is macro-averaged over queries or over the 13 domains**, and the two readings imply different optimisation targets.

## The averaging conflict is the single highest-priority open item

Two independent pieces of evidence point in opposite directions, and both are credible. The RETECO evaluation page states for Track 1a that "nDCG@10 is computed independently for **each query** and macro-averaged," for Track 1b that "step-specific nDCG@10 values are first averaged over the supplied steps for each query, **then aggregated across queries**," and that "per-domain results are also reported **diagnostically**" ([Evaluation page](https://datascienceuibk.github.io/RETECO/evaluation)). The proposal PDF corroborates the diagnostic framing: "each track ranks on a single official metric (nDCG@10) for leaderboard clarity" with "per-domain" analyses "reported separately" ([proposal PDF](https://datascienceuibk.github.io/RETECO/assets/papers/RETECO_SemEval_2027_Proposal.pdf)). That is explicit, official prose, and it says query-macro.

The counter-evidence is arithmetic and comes from your own project notes rather than from a source this research pass re-verified. The starter kit at commit `23093c3` reportedly states in `BASELINE_RESULTS.md` that results are "Macro-averaged over domains, as both papers do," and the 13 published per-domain 1a-train values average to **0.08785**, which rounds to the published **0.0879** ([task site figures](https://datascienceuibk.github.io/RETECO/)). An arithmetic match to four decimals is not weak evidence — it is the kind of coincidence that does not happen by accident. So the most likely reconciliation is not that one side is wrong about the world, but that the *published baseline table* was computed domain-macro while the *leaderboard scorer described on the evaluation page* is query-macro. That reconciliation is a hypothesis, not a finding, and nothing in the notes settles it.

The practical consequence is not cosmetic. Under domain-macro, every domain is worth exactly 1/13: **IOTA with roughly 10 queries counts as much as History with 801** ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)), so effort belongs on the small weak domains, and this is precisely where the organizers' own fusion paper landed its biggest gains — **workplace 0.247 → 0.370 and genealogy 0.310 → 0.363**, which is why its 13-task mean gain (+0.032) is roughly 2.5× its per-query mean gain (+0.013) ([arXiv 2608.08940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08940)). Under query-macro, those same small-domain wins are nearly invisible and the large domains dominate. The two readings therefore recommend opposite allocations of a scarce GPU budget. Note also that this exact +0.032-vs-+0.013 divergence is itself a measurement of how much the two averaging schemes disagree on a real TEMPO system: **the choice is worth about 2 nDCG points of apparent gain.** Resolve it by emailing the lead organizer (abdelrahman.abdallah@uibk.ac.at) and by re-reading `official_baseline.py` in the starter kit to see which aggregation the shipped scorer actually performs. Until then, build the evaluation harness to report *both* numbers on every run — that costs nothing and makes the eventual answer a config flag rather than a re-run.

One mitigating fact reduces the stakes slightly: the ~350 Track 1 test queries are "stratified across all 13 TEMPO domains" ([Task page](https://datascienceuibk.github.io/RETECO/task)), roughly 27 per domain. If stratification means uniform, the two averages converge almost exactly. If it means proportional-to-train, they diverge as badly as on train. The stratum sizes are not published.

## H2 is not novel as a technique, and the reframe is the setting plus the weight sweep

The claim "untested in the literature" is false and must come out of the writeup before submission. ReDI decomposes a query into targeted sub-queries, enriches each with semantic interpretations, retrieves independently per sub-query, and fuses into a final ranking, reporting **30.8 average nDCG@10 on BRIGHT with BM25** — above GPT-4 (27.0) and its own teacher DeepSeek-R1 (29.2) ([arXiv 2509.06544](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.06544v1)). MMLF does the same with pseudo-document expansion and RRF, reporting about **4% average and 8% maximum gains** on Recall@1k and nDCG@10 across five BEIR datasets ([ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-naacl.367/)). Both are one obvious search away for any reviewer. Behind them sits a classical tradition of fusing query variations with RRF and CombSUM that long predates the LLM era ([TOIS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3345001)).

The genuinely dangerous paper is the stage-aware study, whose abstract states that "decomposition during initial retrieval frequently harms retrieval performance due to semantic dilution, yet substantially improves reranking by enabling more fine-grained constraint verification," and which therefore keeps the monolithic query in stage one and uses sub-queries only at rerank time ([arXiv 2606.08577](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08577)). A framing that does not engage this will look uninformed.

But the negatives and positives split along a clean line, and that line is your reframe. **Every clear negative is a replacement of the full query; every clear positive is an augmentation of it.** TEMPO's own ablation is the cleanest instance: **Step-Only averages 14.6 nDCG@10, Query+Step 26.4, Query+All 25.9, with ReasonIR gaining +18 points** from step-only to query-augmented ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). ReDI's concatenation condition — one long merged query — is its worst, for the same stated reason of diluted semantic focus. The second discriminating variable is enrichment: both positives expand each sub-query before retrieving (pseudo-documents in MMLF, interpretations in ReDI), while bare fragment text is the failure mode. So the defensible configuration is full-query ranking **plus** weighted step rankings, score-sum primary and RRF as baseline — which is exactly what ReDI already found best, and which you should report as a confirmation-or-refutation rather than a discovery.

What daylight remains is narrower but real, and it is worth stating precisely in the paper. First, no paper found fuses **organizer-supplied gold reasoning-step decompositions** rather than model-generated sub-queries — and RETECO hands you 3,976 gold steps as sub-track 1b *input*, which removes the decomposition-quality confound that every prior paper carries. Second, no fusion study exists in the **temporal** reasoning-retrieval setting or on TEMPO. Third, nobody has measured the **1b → 1a transfer**: what step-level ranking quality actually buys query-level ranking, when step-level ranking is itself a first-class evaluated task. Fourth — and this is the one concrete measurement gap the prior-art sweep could not close — **no prior work reports a tuned sweep of the weight between the parent-query list and the step lists.** That is a small, honest, executable contribution. Frame H2 as a replication-plus-extension, cite ReDI, MMLF and the stage-aware paper explicitly, and the reviewer risk drops from high to routine.

## The organizers' +0.005 gate tells you where not to spend effort

"Difficulty-Gated Fusion of Reasoning Views for Temporal Retrieval" (CIKM '26) is co-authored by Abdallah and Jatowt, two of the five RETECO organizers, and it is a method paper on TEMPO itself ([arXiv 2608.08940](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08940)). It expands each query into K temporal reasoning views generated once offline by GPT-4o — change-over-time, compare-baseline, event-ordering — retrieves each with a frozen encoder, z-normalises per view, and fuses with per-query weights from a ~1,000-parameter MLP gate regressed onto true per-view nDCG@10 using eight query-performance-prediction features. No reranking, no fine-tuning, no labels at inference, no LLM call at retrieval time. Gains run **+2.3 to +6.3 points** across six backbones, largest on the weakest ones: Contriever 19.6 → 25.9, E5 27.1 → 30.3.

Read the SFR ablation ladder closely, because it is the most actionable table in the entire evidence base: original query K=1 **0.265** → equal-weight fusion K=5 **0.284** → difficulty-gated K=5 **0.289** → difficulty-gated K=3 **0.297**, against a per-query oracle at K=5 of **0.364**. Decompose that. Fusion itself is worth **+0.019**. The learned gate adds **+0.005 at matched K=5** — statistically real (paired bootstrap over n=1,730, mean per-query Δ=+0.013, 95% CI [+0.006, +0.020], P(Δ≤0)=0.0003) but tiny. Tuning K from 5 down to 3 is worth **+0.008**, more than the gate. And the oracle gap of **~0.067** dwarfs all of it, which is why the authors name per-query view *selection* as their own next step.

Three conclusions follow, and they should govern your build order. **Do not build a clever gate.** The sophisticated machinery in the organizers' own paper buys a third of what simply choosing the right number of views buys, and you are competing for the same points with a fraction of their compute. **Do sweep K and the fusion weights** — cheap, CPU-side, and it is where the measurable gain actually sits. And note the calibration that matters most for ambition-setting: their best fused SFR (0.297) still **does not beat published single-retriever DiVeR at 0.320** ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). Backbone strength beats fusion cleverness on this benchmark.

What the organizers left conspicuously untouched is the more interesting map. Across TEMPO and the fusion paper there is **no reranking of any kind** — despite Abdallah owning DeAR, ASRank, DynRank, BracketRank and RankArena, and having published an EMNLP 2025 Findings analysis of reranking models. There is no retriever fine-tuning on in-domain temporal data (their own TempRetriever, WSDM 2026, is cited but never run on TEMPO). There is no hybrid sparse+dense fusion. There is **no use of the passage-level temporal annotations the dataset itself ships** — temporal scope as ISO start/end dates with granularity, tense, confidence. And there is no coverage-aware diversification, despite Temporal Coverage@10 topping out at **72.4%** (ReasonIR), meaning roughly **30% of cross-period queries never retrieve documents spanning all required periods** ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)).

Their thesis statement is worth internalising because it predicts how they will analyse your system: on the downstream RAG evaluation, **no-retrieval (77.3) beat every retriever, including DiVeR (75.6), and only oracle retrieval improved on it (80.5)** — "for complex temporal queries, retrieving wrong temporal evidence is worse than retrieving nothing." This benchmark rewards temporal *precision*, not candidate volume.

## The implementable temporal-IR toolkit is mostly CPU work, and that is a gift

Given a 30-hour weekly GPU quota, the most valuable finding in the temporal-IR literature is that the highest-leverage temporal techniques need no GPU at all. TEMPO's own ablation shows that **stripping temporal signals from queries costs only ~2.2 nDCG points on average**, while a Temporal-Only variant collapses DiVeR from 32.0 to 17.7 ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). The standard reading is "time doesn't matter much." The correct reading is the opposite: current dense retrievers are *barely using* the time tokens, which means the headroom behind an explicitly symbolic temporal signal is larger than the 2.2 suggests. This is corroborated from three independent angles — DiVeR's **Temporal Relevance@10 of only 41.3%**, the Time-Specifier-Merging paper's diagnosis that Contriever and DPR "fail to properly contextualize time-specific information" ([arXiv 2507.06782](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.06782)), and a theoretical result that a fixed embedding dimension cannot realise arbitrary relevance relations, which bites hardest on compositional and constraint queries ([arXiv 2508.21038](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.21038)). There is no single canonical paper proving "embeddings can't tell before-2019 from after-2019"; cite the cluster, not a smoking gun.

The concretely implementable layer is interval reasoning. Berberich et al.'s ECIR 2010 model treats query temporal expressions as first-class evidence and explicitly models the **uncertainty** about which interval an expression like "in the 1990s" denotes ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-642-12275-0_5)). Its modern descendant is Q-TIE (EMNLP 2026 Main), which *learns* the query→⟨t_start, t_end⟩ mapping instead of hand-writing rules and treats the temporal constraint as a separate ranking signal, framed as "a lightweight add-on for temporally-aware RAG pipelines" ([arXiv 2609.23880](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23880)) — though its parameter count and numeric gains are not disclosed in anything the notes could retrieve. Allen interval algebra gives a principled zero-GPU vocabulary for before/after/during/overlaps relations ([IA-RAG, arXiv 2606.06044](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.06044)). Implementing thirteen Allen relations as soft features over (query interval, passage interval) pairs is trivial, deterministic and debuggable.

Crucially, TEMPO ships gold passage-level temporal scope annotations, so you can *validate* a tagger against ground truth rather than trusting it — a rare luxury. What does **not** transfer is the classical recency line: TempRetriever gains **+3.79 nDCG@10 on ArchivalQA and +4.68 on ChroniclingAmericaQA** by fusing document timestamps and query dates ([arXiv 2502.21024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.21024)), but TEMPO's Stack Exchange passages lack reliable publication dates. The usable analogue is parsed *focus time*, not publication time. And do not build on Re3's relevance/recency gating — **the paper has been withdrawn by its authors** ([arXiv 2509.01306](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.01306)).

The one place where the coverage metric and the symbolic layer meet is the clearest unoccupied niche found anywhere in this sweep: a **coverage-aware, precision-oriented reordering of the top ~20** that parses the query's required periods, reads candidate temporal scopes, penalises out-of-window candidates and forces the top-10 to span both the baseline and comparison period for cross-period queries. Trends & Cross-Period Comparison is TEMPO's hardest reasoning class at **mean 17.9 across all models, with even DiVeR at only 23.9**, against an easiest class (Historical Attribution & Context) at mean 35.6 ([TEMPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). TCP is only 154 of 1,730 queries, but it carries disproportionate headroom, costs zero GPU hours to attack, and no published ablation quantifies what such a layer recovers — which means measuring it *is* a contribution.

## The 4B retriever does not fit your budget, so change the question

The model-quality answer is clear and the notes state it cleanly: on BRIGHT with original queries, retriever-only, `hanhainebula/reason-embed-qwen3-4b-0928` reaches **nDCG@10 = 37.1**, versus **31.9** for `AQ-MedAI/Diver-Retriever-4B-1020` — same 4B size, same Apache 2.0 licence, same Qwen3 backbone ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/hanhainebula/reason-embed-qwen3-4b-0928); [arXiv 2510.08252](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08252)). An independent BRIGHT-Pro re-evaluation shifts the absolute numbers but preserves the ordering (BGE-Reasoner-8B 33.8, DIVER-1020 30.6, ReasonIR-8B 24.4, BM25 14.5) ([arXiv 2605.04018](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04018v1)), and the DIVER authors themselves switched their v3 first stage to a BGE-Reasoner-family embedder. Everything at 7–8B is out on hardware grounds: fp16 weights alone are 14–16 GB on a 16 GB card.

Now the arithmetic that overrides all of it. A 4B model at 512 tokens per document costs roughly 4.1 TFLOP per forward pass; at a realistic ~15 effective TFLOPS on a T4 that is 3–4 docs/sec, so **1.65M documents is ~115–150 GPU-hours — four to five weeks of your entire 30-hour quota, for one model, with zero iterations.** The output is **1.65M × 2560-dim fp16 ≈ 8.5 GB** of embeddings (History alone ≈ 1.8 GB), which fits Kaggle's ~20 GB working disk beside the corpus only if each domain is written out as produced. These are estimates, not measurements — no published T4 benchmark exists for any 4B-class embedder, and the notes are explicit that the estimate could be off by a factor of three in either direction.

The recommendation therefore is not "switch models," it is **"measure before you choose, then buy the throughput back."** Run a timed 1,000-document probe as your very first Kaggle session, on both candidates, and simultaneously check for inf/nan on a small batch: these are bf16-native weights and the T4 (CC 7.5) has no bf16, so you load fp16 with an fp32 final norm. Then apply the mitigations, which are worth more than the 5.2-point BRIGHT gap: truncating documents to 256–320 tokens is roughly 2×, length-sorted batching to kill padding waste is 1.3–1.6×, and batching by token budget rather than count compounds both. Reaching ~10 docs/sec brings the full pass to roughly **45 hours — about a week and a half**, which is survivable exactly once. Budget it as a one-time capital expense, cache per-domain fp16 shards forever, and make every subsequent experiment a re-scoring over cached vectors. If the probe says the full pass is unaffordable even after mitigation, the correct fallback is a smaller encoder on all 13 domains rather than a great encoder on some of them, because a missing domain scores zero and — under either averaging rule — that is unrecoverable.

Two caveats keep Diver-1020 alive as a fallback rather than killing it. It is the only one of the two with a **measured TEMPO score (32.0, best in that field)**, and its 40k context exceeds Qwen3's 32k. More importantly, BRIGHT rank does not transfer cleanly to TEMPO: generic E5 (30.4) and SFR (30.0) sit second and third on TEMPO despite being unremarkable on BRIGHT, ReasonIR ties GritLM at 27.2, and the BRIGHT spread of 14.5→38 compresses to 10.8→32 ([tempo-bench.github.io](https://tempo-bench.github.io/)). **ReasonEmbed has no published TEMPO number at all.** So the switch is a well-reasoned bet on a correlated proxy, not a measured improvement on your benchmark — say so in the paper.

On reranking, the evidence supports a firm negative. An MS MARCO cross-encoder on BRIGHT takes BM25 from 14.3 to **13.1 at k=10 and 8.3 at k=100** — monotonically worse the deeper it goes — because MS MARCO teaches topical relevance, the wrong signal when relevance requires reasoning ([BRIGHT, arXiv 2407.12883](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12883v4)). That is strong lineage evidence against `bge-reranker-v2-m3`, though no source reports that specific model on BRIGHT, so it is presumed harmful rather than proven so. Everything that clearly wins is 7B+ (**ReasonRank-7B 35.74**, **Diver-GroupRank-7B 36.65** ([HF card](https://huggingface.co/AQ-MedAI/Diver-GroupRank-7B))) and does not fit. Even a strong reranker gains nearly nothing from depth — GPT-4 goes 17.4 at k=10 to 17.0 at k=100 — while costing 10×. And listwise rerankers, the ones that win on reasoning, are documented to **degrade under temporal distribution shift** via positional bias ([arXiv 2407.06716](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.06716)); nobody has measured the intersection of reasoning-intensive and temporally-shifted, which is exactly where you sit. Spend at most ~2 GPU-hours to falsify the negative: `Qwen/Qwen3-Reranker-4B` (Apache-2.0, ~8 GB fp16) at **k=20, not 100**, with `bge-reranker-v2-m3` in the same harness as a control. If neither beats the un-reranked baseline at k=20, close the question with evidence and never revisit it.

## Conclusion

The three research questions collapse into one strategic fact: **the cheap, published, GPU-free moves dominate the expensive, novel, GPU-hungry ones, and your compute budget makes that alignment lucky rather than limiting.** Query⊕step concatenation is worth +11.8 nDCG points on TEMPO's own ablation and costs nothing. Sweeping K and fusion weights is worth more than the organizers' learned gate. A symbolic interval-overlap and coverage-diversification layer over the top-20 is CPU-only, targets the benchmark's stated failure mode, and is the one technique in this entire sweep that is both aligned with the metric and unoccupied by prior work. The 4B model swap is worth perhaps five BRIGHT points that may not transfer to TEMPO at all, and costs your whole quota for a month. Rank those by points-per-GPU-hour and the build order writes itself.

The deeper reframe concerns what the paper should claim. You cannot claim fusion; ReDI and MMLF own it. You can claim something narrower and more defensible: that in a benchmark which *supplies* gold decompositions and *evaluates* step-level ranking as a first-class task, the parent-query-to-step weighting is a tunable with an unmeasured optimum, and that its optimum interacts with temporal coverage in a way no atemporal fusion study could have observed. That is a smaller claim than "untested in the literature," and it has the considerable advantage of being true. Two things should happen before any code runs: settle the averaging question, because it silently redefines what "improvement" means, and run the throughput probe, because a recommendation that consumes five weeks of a four-month project is not a recommendation.
