# Two defaults, not the hardware, blocked you

**The project is feasible, and by a wide margin.** Embedding the full 1.65M-document Track 1 corpus with `AQ-MedAI/Diver-Retriever-0.6B` costs an estimated **7 GPU-hours** (band 5–12 h) on a single free Kaggle T4 — one session, under a quarter of a week's quota, with enough left over to re-run it twice. The 115–150 GPU-hour figure that made this look impossible was not a measurement of the T4; it was the arithmetic of a 4B model run with two library defaults left in place — **fp32 instead of fp16 (~3×)** and **padding every batch to `max_length` with no length sorting (~3.2× on this corpus's length distribution)**. Remove both and the same 4B model lands at ~56 h derived; swap 4B for the 0.6B sibling in the same DIVER family and it lands at ~7 h, for a **BRIGHT cost of 3.7 nDCG@10 points (28.9 → 25.2)** ([Diver-Retriever-0.6B card](https://huggingface.co/AQ-MedAI/Diver-Retriever-0.6B)). Two premises in the working plan also need correcting: **Kaggle GPU sessions cap at 9 hours, not 12** ([Kaggle: Efficient GPU Usage](https://www.kaggle.com/docs/efficient-gpu-usage)), and the TEMPO Table 3 numbers in the notes were wrong — TEMPO's "E5" is the 7.1B `e5-mistral-7b-instruct`, not a small model. The single most important caveat: **almost every throughput number below is roofline arithmetic, not a measurement.** No public tokens/sec benchmark exists for any modern embedding model on a T4. The first action this week is a 15-minute self-benchmark, not a production run.

## The 115–150 hour estimate was a padding bug and a dtype bug compounding

The original figure came from a defensible chain — 4B model, 512 tokens per document, ~4.1 TFLOP per forward pass, ~15 effective TFLOPS on a T4, 3–4 docs/sec, 1.65M documents. Every step is arithmetically sound. The error is in what "512 tokens per document" means. It silently assumed **1.65M × 512 = 0.845B tokens actually pushed through the GPU**, when the corpus contains only **0.26B real tokens** after a 512 cap and deduplication of the 29.4% byte-identical documents. The mean document is **158 tokens** (0.26B / 1.65M), so padding every batch to 512 makes the GPU compute **3.2× more tokens than exist**. `SentenceTransformer.encode()` sorts by length internally and largely fixes this for free, but only *within* each list you hand it — chunking the corpus into fixed slices and calling `encode` per slice throws most of the win away. The measured corroboration is thin but consistent in direction: "smart batching" is reported at **~2× on a t5-small on a T4 specifically** ([Théo Martin](https://medium.com/@theom/smart-batching-or-how-to-speed-up-your-training-and-inferences-for-free-d7c9a587c63)), and token-count-based batching at **9.15× with up to 40% less padding compute** against a naive fixed-batch baseline ([MongoDB Engineering](https://www.mongodb.com/company/blog/engineering/token-count-based-batching-faster-cheaper-embedding-inference-for-queries)).

The second default is dtype. HuggingFace and sentence-transformers load in **fp32 unless told otherwise**, and the T4 has no TF32 fallback — an fp32 matmul drops off the tensor cores entirely, from **65 TFLOPS fp16 down to 8.1 TFLOPS fp32**. Realized gains in HF are typically 2–3×, not the full 8×, but that is still the largest single line item after padding. This is not theoretical: the AWS T4 BERT work had to set an explicit flag (`MXNET_FC_TRUE_FP16`) to get true fp16 compute at all, direct evidence that frameworks do not take the tensor-core path by default ([AWS ML Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/bert-inference-on-g4-instances-using-apache-mxnet-and-gluonnlp-1-million-requests-for-20-cents/)).

Stacked, here is where the hours actually go for the 4B model, all rows **[DERIVED]** from tokens ÷ throughput:

| Configuration | Tokens actually computed | Est. hours | Multiplier |
|---|---|---|---|
| fp32, pad-to-512, no dedup | 0.845B (×3 for fp32) | **~540 h** | 96× |
| fp16, pad-to-512, no dedup | 0.845B | **~180 h** | 32× |
| fp32, sorted, deduped | 0.26B (×3 for fp32) | **~170 h** | 30× |
| **fp16, sorted, deduped (4B)** | 0.26B | **~56 h** | 10× |
| **fp16, sorted, deduped (0.6B)** | 0.26B | **~7 h** | 1.25× |
| fp16, sorted, deduped (BGE-M3 class) | 0.26B | **~4.5 h** | 1× |

The 115–150 h estimate sits between the second and third rows — exactly where you land if you fix one default and not the other. **The failure mode was never throughput. It was pipeline hygiene.**

## DIVER ships 0.6B and 1.7B, and 0.6B beats every 7B model in the BRIGHT paper

This is the highest-leverage finding in the whole sweep, and it is a model-registry fact rather than an optimisation. The DIVER-Retriever family was released at three sizes, **all Apache-2.0, all BRIGHT-scored**: **0.6B = 25.2, 1.7B = 27.3, 4B = 28.9** nDCG@10 ([Diver-Retriever-1.7B card](https://huggingface.co/AQ-MedAI/Diver-Retriever-1.7B); [DIVER, arXiv:2508.07995](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.07995)). Dropping from 4B to 0.6B costs **3.7 points (−12.8% relative)** for a ~6.7× parameter reduction and roughly an **8× reduction in GPU-hours**; dropping only to 1.7B costs **1.6 points (−5.5%)**. The marginal return per parameter is steeply diminishing above 1.7B, which is the whole argument in one sentence.

The sanity check that should settle any residual doubt: **DIVER-0.6B at 25.2 beats every model in the original BRIGHT paper**, including GTE-Qwen at 7.7B (22.5), GritLM-7B (21.0), SFR-Mistral (18.3) and E5-Mistral-7B (17.9) ([BRIGHT, ICLR 2025, Table 2](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/7a0f8055c838df8e62329a76c7c6403d-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Reasoning-retrieval fine-tuning is worth far more than scale here. The converse is equally sharp and rules out the cheap encoders as a *primary* stage: SBERT (14.9), Instructor-L (14.2) and BGE-large (13.7) all sit within ±0.5 of BM25's **14.5** on BRIGHT, so spending even 1 GPU-hour to embed 1.65M documents with them buys essentially nothing over a free CPU BM25 index.

At 0.6B in fp16 the weights are ~1.2 GB, leaving ~14 GB of a T4 for activations — the hard constraint is not close to binding. Two T4-specific hazards attach to this choice. The DIVER cards specify **BF16, which compute capability 7.5 does not implement** — Turing has no physical BF16 execution units ([vLLM #1400](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/issues/1400)) — so a code path that silently requests bf16 will either error or fall back to fp32 and run at one-eighth speed. And **FlashAttention-2 requires Ampere or newer**; the README says so outright and directs Turing users to FA1 ([flash-attention #1608](https://github.com/Dao-AILab/flash-attention/issues/1608)). Use `model.half()` under `torch.inference_mode()` and let PyTorch SDPA dispatch to its memory-efficient backend; HF's own TEI turns Flash Attention **off by default on the Turing image because it suffers precision issues** ([TEI README](https://github.com/huggingface/text-embeddings-inference/blob/main/README.md)), so FA1 is not worth the numerical risk.

## The T4 is compute-bound, so truncation and padding are two different levers

The intuition that shortening `max_length` is the main cost lever is wrong, and the reason matters for how you spend the next week. The T4's ridge point is **65e12 FLOPS ÷ 320e9 B/s = 203 FLOP/byte**. For a batched encoder forward pass, arithmetic intensity equals the number of tokens in the batch — at batch 32 × seq 256 that is 8,192 FLOP/byte, **40× past the ridge**. The 320 GB/s memory figure never binds. Throughput therefore tracks **non-embedding parameter count**, not sequence length.

Concretely, for a BERT-base-shaped encoder the per-token FFN and projection cost is ~1.7e8 FLOPs while the attention-score term is ~4.4e4 × n. At n=512 attention is **~12% of the linear term**; at n=128 it is ~3%. **Cutting `max_length` from 512 to 128 saves roughly 9% of compute per token, not 4×.** [DERIVED — no measured tokens/sec-vs-max_length curve exists for an embedding model on a T4.]

These are the two separate effects, and conflating them is what produces bad plans:

**Effect one — padding waste, which sorting fixes and costs nothing.** With a 512 cap and no sorting you compute 512 tokens for a 158-token mean document: a flat 3.2× tax. Global length sorting plus token-budget batching removes almost all of it, with **zero quality cost**. This is the lever.

**Effect two — total real tokens, which truncation genuinely reduces.** From your own measurements, the corpus is 0.81B tokens raw, **0.36B at a 512 cap and 0.29B at 256** — so 512 retains 44.4% of tokens and 256 retains 35.8%, making **512 cost 1.24× the 256 cap**. That 24% is real and it is additive to the sorting win, but it is a corpus-semantics decision, not a GPU-physics one. Keep 512: it is the training length of the encoder class, it is lossless through roughly p90 in the shorter domains (median is 104–163 words), and models evaluated beyond their training window measurably degrade ([LongEmbed, arXiv:2404.12096](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.12096v3)). 256 is a defensible fallback that buys only 24%.

Two policies follow and both are cheap. **Deduplicate first** — 29.4% byte-identical duplicates is a 29.4% saving at exactly zero quality cost, strictly better than anything truncation offers. And **pre-slice raw text to `max_length × 6` characters before tokenising**: HF fast tokenizers encode the entire input and truncate afterwards, an open and acknowledged issue ([huggingface/tokenizers #1573](https://github.com/huggingface/tokenizers/issues/1573)), with measured scaling of 650,000 characters → **8.98 seconds** in the tokenizer alone ([transformers #27187](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers/issues/27187)). Your 1,513,968-word document would otherwise cost minutes of pure CPU waste and is a plausible cause of an apparent hang. This is a mandatory guard, not an optimisation.

Do **not** chunk the whole corpus. Unconditional chunking is ~2.25× the 512-cap cost for a published benefit of **+0.5 nDCG@10 for the dense retriever and −0.1 for BM25** (DIVER's DChunk ablation on BRIGHT StackExchange: DIVER-Retriever 37.5 → 38.0, BM25 37.1 → 37.0) ([DIVER, arXiv:2508.07995](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.07995v1)). That is the worst GPU-hours-per-point trade available. Chunk only the p99 tail, capped at 8 chunks per document, scored MAX over chunks, and leave the sparse index on truncated text.

## Corrected TEMPO table, and why the cheapest win is query-side

The TEMPO numbers circulating in the notes (SBERT 22.4, BGE 19.9, E5 27.1, SFR 26.5) **do not appear in the paper's Table 3**. Only BM25 10.8 and DiVeR 32.0 match. Here is Table 3 as transcribed from the paper, NDCG@10 averaged over 13 domains ([TEMPO, arXiv:2601.09523](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)):

| Model | TEMPO nDCG@10 | Size class per paper | Actual checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| BM25 | **10.8** | sparse | — |
| Contriever | **21.4** | <1B | `facebook/contriever`, 110M |
| BGE | **22.0** | <1B | **BGE-M3, 568M** (not bge-large) |
| Qwen | **22.8** | >1B | GTE-Qwen |
| Rader | **24.2** | reasoning, >1B | — |
| Inst-L | **24.8** | <1B | `hkunlp/instructor-large`, 335M |
| SBERT | **24.9** | <1B | `all-mpnet-base-v2`, 109M |
| GritLM | **27.2** | >1B | GritLM-7B |
| ReasonIR | **27.2** | reasoning, >1B | ReasonIR-8B |
| SFR | **30.0** | >1B | SFR-Embedding-Mistral, 7.1B |
| **E5** | **30.4** | **>1B** | **`e5-mistral-7b-instruct`, 7.1B** |
| DiVeR | **32.0** | reasoning, >1B | checkpoint size not stated |

Two things change once the table is right. First, **the "small E5" shortcut does not exist** — TEMPO's E5 is a 7.1B decoder that the paper itself files under ">1B", and 7–8B fp16 weights are 14–16 GB, which does not fit a 16 GB T4 without quantization that runs *slower* per FLOP on Turing. Rule that class out entirely. Second, the sub-1B gap on TEMPO is much narrower than on BRIGHT: SBERT at 109M scores 24.9 against DiVeR's 32.0, a 7.1-point gap, and it actually **beats GTE-Qwen-7B (22.8) and Rader (24.2)**. Note also that "BGE" means different checkpoints in the two benchmarks — bge-large-en-v1.5 on BRIGHT, BGE-M3 on TEMPO — so those rows must never be merged.

Now the reframe that should change where your GPU hours go. **TEMPO Table 5 reports that adding explicit temporal-intent tags to queries moves ReasonIR from 27.3 to 35.3 — a +8.0 nDCG@10 gain** ([TEMPO Table 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). That is **more than twice the entire 4B→0.6B DIVER penalty**, and it scales with the **~1,730 query count, not the 1.65M document count**. A comparable signal appears in the query-reformulation experiment: ReasonIR gains +13.7 with GPT-4o reasoning expansion (27.3 → 41.0), BGE gains modestly (22.0 → ~26), DiVeR is flat, and **BM25 collapses (10.8 → 4.3–6.2)** because reasoning text dilutes lexical signals ([TEMPO Figure 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)). The strategic conclusion is blunt: the corpus pass is a one-time capital expense you should make as cheap as possible and then never repeat, and the iteration budget belongs on the query side, where every experiment costs minutes instead of hours. Also worth knowing before over-investing in the retriever at all — on TEMPO's RAG evaluation, **the no-retrieval baseline scores 77.3 answer correctness against DiVeR-retrieval's 75.6 and BGE's 74.8** ([TEMPO Table 6](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.09523)): current retrievers actively hurt downstream QA on this benchmark.

## The plan for this week, in order

**Correct the session premise first.** GPU sessions cap at **9 hours**, not 12 — 12 is the CPU-only limit ([Kaggle: Efficient GPU Usage](https://www.kaggle.com/docs/efficient-gpu-usage)). The weekly quota is "30 hours or sometimes higher", floating with demand ([Kaggle general/135810](https://www.kaggle.com/general/135810); [product-feedback/173129](https://www.kaggle.com/product-feedback/173129)), so plan against 30 and treat anything more as luck. Budget ~8h of compute per session with a hard stop to leave room for the dataset push, and use **"Save & Run All"** commits so the job runs headless and survives the idle timeout.

**Step 1 — CPU-only session, costs zero GPU quota.** Deduplicate, pre-slice to 3,072 characters, tokenize once, store `input_ids` and token counts, sort globally by length, and freeze the order into a manifest (`doc_id → global_position → shard_id`) at ~50k documents per shard, ~33 shards. Publish as a private Kaggle Dataset. Sorting and sharding must agree — define shards over the *sorted* order, or the length-batching win and the resume logic fight each other, and a later re-sort with a different tokenizer version silently corrupts resume.

**Step 2 — the self-benchmark, ~1 GPU-hour, and this is the first thing you run.** Take a stratified 20,000-document sample including p90 and p99 documents. Print `model.dtype` before encoding and confirm it is `torch.float16` — that one line is the highest-value assertion in the script. Check for inf/nan on a small batch, since the DIVER weights are bf16-native and you are loading fp16. Binary-search batch size until OOM and back off ~20%; log `torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated()`. Time DIVER-0.6B, and if time permits 1.7B, at fp16 with token-budget batching. **Then replace every derived number in this report with yours.** Also check whether the **T4×2** option bills at 1× or 2× the quota rate — if 1×, sharding across both GPUs is a free 2× on wall-clock, and that single measurement determines the whole schedule. [UNVERIFIED and not documented anywhere.]

**Step 3 — production.** One 8-hour session should finish DIVER-0.6B at the derived rate. Per shard: encode fp16 → `np.save` array + IDs parquet → fsync → write a `.done` sentinel only after both are flushed. Resume by listing sentinels, never by checking whether the `.npy` exists — a killed session leaves truncated files. Storage at 1024 dims fp16 is **3.38 GB** for 1.65M documents, trivially inside the ~20 GB working disk and the 200 GB dataset quota ([Kaggle product announcement](https://www.kaggle.com/product-announcements/512322)). Keep fp16 as the archival artifact and derive INT8 index vectors later on CPU — INT8 output quantization costs **−1.95 nDCG@10 alone but only −0.09 with rescoring** ([Vespa](https://blog.vespa.ai/embedding-tradeoffs-quantified/)) and is regenerable if the A/B goes badly.

**Step 4 — spend the remaining ~20 hours on queries, not documents.** Temporal-intent tagging and step-wise query construction, per the +8.0 result above.

**Do not** adopt TEI or vLLM: both technically support Turing but add Rust/Docker build burden inside Kaggle for batching logic you can write in thirty lines, and **no T4 throughput benchmark exists for either**. Do not chase TensorRT INT8 unless step 2 says you are over quota — the T4 ladder there (ONNX fp32 14.93 ms → TRT fp16 8.12 ms → TRT INT8 4.82 ms, i.e. ~3.1×) comes from a community tutorial, not a vendor benchmark ([Markaicode](https://markaicode.com/tutorial/tensorrt-quantization-tutorial/)), and engine builds must be rebuilt per shape profile and per session. `torch.compile` gives at most 1.2–1.4× ([HF docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/en/perf_torch_compile)) and **recompiles on every shape**, which directly fights dynamic padding.

It belongs in the 350M compute class, identical to bge-large, not the 560M class.

## Conclusion

The reframe worth carrying forward is that **on a fixed free-tier budget the binding constraint is rarely the hardware — it is the compounding of defaults nobody audited.** A 96× spread separates the worst configuration (fp32, pad-to-512, no dedup, 4B) from the best (fp16, sorted, deduped, 0.6B), and only one factor of eight in that spread comes from the model choice. The other twelve come from three lines of code. That ratio is why the self-benchmark outranks every other action: it is the only step that converts this entire report from arithmetic into fact, and it costs one of thirty hours.

The second implication is architectural. Once the corpus pass costs 7 hours instead of 150, embedding stops being a strategic decision and becomes a fixed cost you pay once and cache forever — which frees the quota for the place where TEMPO says the points actually are. A +8.0 gain from tagging 1,730 queries dominates a 3.7-point loss from shrinking the encoder by 6.7×, and it is iterable in minutes rather than sessions. The correct posture for the rest of the project is a cheap, permanently cached 0.6B first stage plus aggressive query-side work, with DIVER-1.7B (27.3 BRIGHT, ~21 h derived) held in reserve as the upgrade to spend a spare week on if, and only if, a measured rate says you can afford it.
